Why your AI uses the right rule at the wrong time.

Teaching an AI assistant a personal rule takes ten seconds. Teaching it when to keep that rule to itself is one of AI’s sneakiest problems. Here is the whole story in pictures, plus a live simulator you can click through yourself.

Small AIs need a strict filter. Big AIs need you not to hide anything from them.

by Idan Shimon · October 2026

Scene 02 · The Mental Model

Your AI does not have a single “memory.” It has a desk.

When you save a rule in an AI app, you are not rewiring the AI’s brain. Every time you ask a question, the app prepares a fresh packet of paper, (called a prompt) and hands it to the AI.

The takeaway: most people blame the AI Writer when a rule misfires, but the real culprit is almost always Job #2: the Bouncer put the wrong sticky notes on the desk.
Scene 03 · Two Ways to Pick a Note

The Word-Match Spotlight vs. The 769-Number Bouncer.

How does an app decide whether to hand a sticky note to the AI? Most apps use a Spotlight: it grabs any note that sounds similar to your question. We built a small research app called Morrow to test a Learned Bouncer instead.

Why “769”? The app turns every sentence into a list of 768 numbers that capture its meaning. For each note, the Bouncer learns one weight per number plus one cutoff: 769 numbers, trained in a few milliseconds.

Scene 05 · The Plot Twist

Why the Bouncer helped the Small AI, but hurt the Big AI.

If you clicked the purple “Break the Bouncer” button above and switched to the Big AI, you just discovered the biggest surprise of our 216-trial study.

The lesson: small AIs fail when you give them extra notes they don’t need. Big AIs fail when you hide a note they do need.

Bouncer 33 / 36 (91.7%)

When we later tuned the Spotlight’s cutoff on a new 24-question test, Spotlight jumped from 4.2% (1/24) to 70.8% (17/24), while the Bouncer scored 83.3% (20/24).

Update: a newer small AI changes the picture

We gave the same 108 questions to Gemma 4 E4B, a small model from Google (about 4B, April 2026). Same notes, same grading. It ran on one cloud graphics card.

Small AI, 2024 (llama 3.2 3B) · no notes / Spotlight / Bouncer3 · 15 · 18
Small AI, 2026 (Gemma 4 E4B) · no notes / Spotlight / Bouncer12 · 36 · 30
Big AI (GPT-5.6) · no notes / Spotlight / Bouncer15 · 36 · 33

Correct answers out of 36 in each case.

What it means: the newer small model behaves like the big one. Extra notes did not confuse it (36 of 36), and it lost points under the Bouncer on the same reworded task where the Bouncer hid the needed note (3 runs). It lost 3 more runs on format: it wrapped the code in backticks. So the rule above is about how well a model ignores clutter, not only about its size. A 2026 small model already does.
The direction: small models are catching up with the big ones. With the same notes, the 2026 small model matched the big model with Spotlight (36 of 36) and came within 3 answers with the Bouncer (30 versus 33). The 2024 small model got half. Its answers were just as short, about 12 words, and most came back in under a second on a cloud graphics card. One honest note: Gemma 4 E4B uses about 4B numbers per word but stores about 8B in total, so it needs more memory than llama 3.2 3B.

Scene 06 · What To Remember

Three rules of thumb for anyone using or building AI.

1. Give “No” examples, not just rules

If a rule uses common words like greeting, summary, or archive, show when not to use it. Without negative examples, word-matching will eventually fire at the wrong time.

2. Match the filter to the brain size

Small AIs get confused by extra notes, so a strict Bouncer helps them. Big AIs ignore extra notes easily, so make sure your filter never hides a secret rule they need.

3. Keep an instant off-switch

Because Morrow keeps rules as plain text in a notebook and stores only 769 numbers per rule, you can pause (revoke) or delete (erase) a bad rule mid-conversation without retraining the AI.
